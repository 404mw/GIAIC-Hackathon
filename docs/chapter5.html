Chapter 5: Sensing the World: Computer Vision and Sensor Fusion

A robot's ability to act intelligently is fundamentally limited by its ability to perceive its environment accurately. Just as humans rely on their senses, robots rely on a suite of sensors to gather information about the world. This chapter covers the primary sensors used in modern robotics, with a focus on computer vision and the critical process of sensor fusion.

The Robot's Senses: Key Sensor Modalities​

1. Vision (Cameras)​

Cameras are the richest source of data for a robot, providing high-resolution information about the appearance of the world. They are passive sensors (they only capture existing light) and are essential for tasks like object recognition, navigation, and human-robot interaction.

  • Monocular Cameras: A single camera, similar to a single human eye. It provides rich 2D information but struggles to perceive depth accurately.
  • Stereo Cameras: A pair of cameras separated by a known distance. By comparing the two images (stereopsis), the robot can compute a depth map, enabling 3D perception.

2. Proprioception (Encoders)​

Proprioception is the sense of self-movement and body position. In robots, this is achieved with encoders, which are sensors that measure the position of the robot's joints. Encoders provide the robot with direct knowledge of its own configuration (e.g., "my elbow is bent at a 90-degree angle").

3. Depth and Distance (LiDAR and Depth Cameras)​

While vision is rich, other sensors provide more direct and accurate 3D measurements.

  • LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging): An active sensor that emits laser beams and measures the time it takes for the light to reflect off objects. This produces a highly accurate 3D "point cloud" of the environment. LiDAR is a key sensor for autonomous navigation.
  • Depth Cameras: These cameras, like the Intel RealSense or Microsoft Kinect, project an infrared pattern onto the scene and measure its distortion to calculate depth for each pixel. They provide a dense depth map similar to a stereo camera but often in a smaller and cheaper package.

4. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs)​

An IMU is a small chip that measures a robot's orientation and angular velocity. It typically contains:

  • An Accelerometer: Measures linear acceleration (and gravity).
  • A Gyroscope: Measures rotational velocity.

IMUs are critical for balance and stabilization, especially in dynamic robots like humanoids.

The Brain's Visual Cortex: Computer Vision​

Raw sensor data is not enough; the robot needs to interpret it. Computer Vision is the field of AI dedicated to helping computers "see" and understand the content of digital images and videos. In robotics, key computer vision tasks include:

  • Object Detection: Identifying and drawing a bounding box around objects in an image (e.g., "there is a cup at this location").
  • Image Segmentation: Classifying each pixel in an image to a specific category (e.g., "these pixels are part of the floor, these are a table").
  • Pose Estimation: Determining the 3D position and orientation of an object (or the robot itself) relative to the camera.

The deep learning revolution has had a massive impact on computer vision. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are now the standard for most vision tasks, enabling robots to perceive the world with unprecedented accuracy.

Sensor Fusion: Creating a Coherent Worldview​

No single sensor is perfect. Cameras are sensitive to lighting conditions, LiDAR has a limited field of view, and IMUs can drift over time. Sensor Fusion is the process of combining data from multiple sensors to create a more accurate and robust understanding of the environment than any single sensor could provide on its own.

A common algorithm used for sensor fusion is the Kalman Filter. It is a mathematical tool that uses a series of measurements observed over time, containing statistical noise and other inaccuracies, and produces estimates of unknown variables that tend to be more accurate than those based on a single measurement alone.

Example: By fusing the data from an IMU and wheel encoders, a mobile robot can get a much more accurate estimate of its position than it could from either sensor individually. The encoders provide good short-term odometry, while the IMU helps to correct for drift and orientation errors.

In the next chapter, we will turn our attention from sensing to acting, exploring the actuators and control systems that allow a robot to move and interact with the world.
